## How many pedestrians are killed on India's roads?

MoRTH's police returns record **36,526 pedestrians killed in road crashes in 2024**. That is one in five of the 177,175 people recorded as killed on India's roads, and close to 100 pedestrian deaths a day in a leap year. Only two-wheeler users form a larger victim group in this national table.

In June 2026, after the deaths counted here, the [Supreme Court recognised walking as a fundamental right](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2024/42514/42514_2024_3_1501_71888_Judgement_19-Jun-2026.pdf). In *Maniyar Iliyaz v. P. Ayyappan*, the Court said that this right includes access to demarcated footpaths and takes priority over the movement of motor vehicles. The ruling followed the death of a five-year-old boy struck by a tanker while walking to school where there was neither a footpath nor a pedestrian crossing. It gives the national death count a sharper civic question: what space had been made for the people expected to walk?

A pedestrian is the person recorded as walking when they were killed. The label does not describe who caused the collision. It also does not tell us how dangerous a walk is compared with a ride or drive. To answer that, we would need deaths divided by the number or distance of walking journeys, alongside the same measure for other forms of travel. The report does not provide those denominators. The count still matters: it says that walking is involved in a large share of the road-death record, even before we know the chance of harm on any one trip. The [broader road-death article](/articles/who-dies-on-indias-roads/) places this toll beside the other road-user groups.

## Is the pedestrian toll falling?

No, at least in the latest comparison the report provides. Recorded pedestrian deaths rose from 35,221 in 2023 to 36,526 in 2024, an increase of 1,305 people or 3.7 per cent. Pedestrians made up 20.4 per cent of reported road deaths in 2023 and 20.6 per cent in 2024. The share moved little while the number of people lost rose.

Two years do not establish a persistent trend. Counts can move with travel volume, crash severity and reporting. A longer series would require joining annual reports carefully. MoRTH says it changed the format for identifying victim road users from 2019. Earlier reports had classified deaths primarily by the impacting vehicle. We should not splice pre-2019 labels into this series as though they meant the same thing. This article stays with the 2024 victim data and the report's direct 2023 comparison.

## How old were the pedestrians who died?

The largest published age band is 45–60, with 8,436 pedestrian deaths. Another 6,160 victims were 60 or older, and 1,888 were younger than 18. The age of 792 pedestrians was recorded as unknown. The chart leaves that group visible. These counts sum to all 36,526 recorded pedestrian deaths.

The distribution resists a simple story that walking deaths belong to only one stage of life. Children, working-age adults and older people all appear. The report's age bands are broad, so we cannot see what happened at a particular birthday or estimate the risk of a school walk. Nor can we infer that a 60-year-old faces more danger per kilometre walked simply because an age band has more recorded deaths. Groups differ in size and in how much they walk. The next comparison asks a different, narrower question: among people killed on roads at each age, how many were pedestrians?

## Are older road victims more often pedestrians?

Yes, as a share of **recorded road deaths within the same age band**. Of the 14,767 people aged 60 and over killed in road crashes in 2024, 6,160 were pedestrians. That is 41.7 per cent. Among victims aged 18–25, 4,333 of 34,154 were pedestrians, or 12.7 per cent. The bars show that walking takes a different place in the victim mix at different ages.

The calculation matters. For each age band we divided pedestrian deaths by *all* road deaths in that band, then multiplied by 100. The denominator changes from band to band. A larger percentage among older victims could reflect how people travel, what happens when they are struck, or both. It is **not** the probability that an older person dies while walking. That would require counts of older people walking, their journeys or distance, and comparable exposure for other ages. The report supplies none of those.

## Were the pedestrians killed mostly men?

The police record lists 29,055 male and 7,471 female pedestrian victims in 2024. Men therefore account for 79.5 per cent of the recorded pedestrian deaths. Those figures come from the age-and-sex annexure and also appear in the report's own narrative, giving us a useful cross-check on a scanned table.

The split tells us who appears in the death record. It cannot tell us whether walking is more dangerous for a man than for a woman. We do not know how many trips each group made, when and where they walked, or how often they crossed high-traffic roads. It would be especially misleading to turn 79.5 per cent into a claim about who behaves carefully. The victim's sex is not a crash-cause field. The next table changes perspective again, from the person who died to the vehicle police recorded in the collision.

## What vehicles were recorded in pedestrian deaths?

Two-wheelers were the largest named impacting-vehicle group, recorded in 10,378 pedestrian deaths. Cars, taxis, vans and light motor vehicles appear in 9,302; trucks and lorries in 5,165. The “Others” category holds 7,857 deaths, more than one in five pedestrian victims. It mixes vehicle and other classifications, so it is a substantial blind spot rather than a single kind of crash.

MoRTH calls the recorded counterpart a “crime vehicle” in its table. The term is administrative. A row saying a pedestrian was struck by a two-wheeler is not a legal verdict about the rider. The national table does not record the street layout, crossing, speed or sequence of actions. Nor do the bars rank how dangerous each vehicle is per kilometre driven. They help identify which encounters need closer local study. A useful investigation would connect the crash record to the actual place where people crossed and vehicles moved.

## Where are the largest pedestrian death totals?

Tamil Nadu reports 4,712 pedestrian deaths in 2024, followed by Bihar with 4,149, Maharashtra with 3,344 and West Bengal with 3,241. The ten states shown in the chart account for 27,354 deaths, 74.9 per cent of the national pedestrian total. The remaining 9,172 deaths are spread across other states and union territories. Each displayed value was checked against the original scanned annexure; a second annexure's age-and-sex rows reconcile with the state totals.

These are counts of recorded loss, useful for deciding where more detailed reporting and investigation are needed. They are not a league table of how safe it is to walk. States differ in population, how much people walk, road networks, traffic and completeness of police reporting. Even a per-resident rate would miss differences in walking exposure. To compare the danger of a walking journey across states, we would need a consistent record of walking trips and pedestrian deaths. The national report provides the second half of that calculation, imperfectly, and not the first.

## Is this only a national-highway problem?

No. MoRTH records 11,386 pedestrian deaths on national highways in 2024, 31.2 per cent of the country's pedestrian total. Subtracting that count from 36,526 leaves 25,140 on **all other roads combined**. That second bar includes state highways and other roads; the source table does not break the pedestrian remainder into those categories here.

The larger off-highway count matters because a highway-only response would miss most recorded pedestrian deaths. It does not prove that another road type is more dangerous for a person walking. Highways and local roads carry very different volumes of traffic and pedestrian movement. The report gives no common exposure measure to divide the deaths by. The split points towards a broad street-and-road question: people walking need protection in many settings, and the national total alone does not tell us which crossing or stretch should be fixed first.

## What does the right to walk require?

The [Supreme Court judgment](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2024/42514/42514_2024_3_1501_71888_Judgement_19-Jun-2026.pdf) identifies a duty that goes with the right. Where there is a road, urban development authorities, municipal bodies and panchayats must work to demarcate, build, maintain and protect footpaths and other pedestrian infrastructure. The Court said people can seek legal remedies when that duty is breached. It also called for a dedicated legal framework; the judgment itself did not establish a new regulator or measure how many Indian roads have usable footpaths.

The national MoRTH tables used here do not identify a single cause of pedestrian deaths. They do not tell us whether each victim had a usable footpath or where the nearest safe crossing was. Nor do they give a reliable national measure of vehicle speed at the crash or tell us whether a later hospital death was linked back to it. Reading the impacting-vehicle row as a verdict on behaviour would skip the place where the collision happened. The 2024 figures describe deaths recorded **before** the 2026 ruling. They cannot test whether authorities have since complied with it.

The World Health Organization (WHO) [pedestrian-safety manual](https://www.who.int/publications/b/65858) describes the evidence behind separating pedestrians from traffic, reducing conflict at intersections and managing speed through road design, enforcement and other measures. Those are general findings, not measurements of India's 2024 streets or proof that a named measure would have prevented every death in this report. For an Indian city or district, the practical next step is to document the footpaths and crossings people can actually use. Then connect crash records to road geometry, pedestrian movement, traffic speed and care after a crash. A national annual PDF can locate the burden. It cannot tell us which street has fulfilled the right to walk.

## How should you read these figures?

The death counts come from MoRTH's *Road Accidents in India 2024*, assembled from state and union-territory police returns. Table 4.4 supplies the 2023–24 pedestrian totals and road-user comparison. Annexure 33 supplies pedestrian age and sex; Table 4.2 supplies all road deaths by age for the within-age percentages. Table 4.5 and Annexure 29(a) classify the vehicles recorded in pedestrian collisions and give state totals. Table 2.11 supplies pedestrian deaths on national highways. We used the original PDF, checked every plotted observation, and reconciled age, sex and state totals. The scanned annexures were read with a model only as a transcription aid; the displayed cells were checked against the original page images.

The legal account comes from the Supreme Court's [19 June 2026 judgment in *Maniyar Iliyaz v. P. Ayyappan*](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2024/42514/42514_2024_3_1501_71888_Judgement_19-Jun-2026.pdf), especially its conclusions on pages 12–13. It establishes a right and corresponding duties; it is not a source for the 2024 death counts, the prevalence of footpaths or the effect of a safety measure.

Shares of all 2024 road deaths divide by **177,175**. Pedestrian-only shares divide by **36,526**. The age-composition chart divides pedestrian deaths in each age band by all road deaths in that *same* band. “Other roads combined” is 36,526 minus 11,386 national-highway pedestrian deaths. None of these operations yields deaths per walking journey or per kilometre walked. The 2023–24 comparison is short by design because victim classification changed in 2019. WHO's manual provides general evidence on pedestrian protection, not an India-specific exposure denominator. Police reporting can miss deaths, especially if a later hospital death is not linked back to the crash record.
