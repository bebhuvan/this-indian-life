## Why is 'Has India ended extreme poverty?' the wrong question to ask in 2026?

India's poverty story changes with the line you draw. In the World Bank's *Poverty, Prosperity, and Planet Report 2026*, the estimated share living below $3 a person a day fell from 49.38% in 1990 to **2.29% in 2024**. At $8.30 a day, 78.78% remained below the line. Both estimates come from the same consumption distribution. One records the retreat of extreme poverty; the other records how far most households remain from the living standards used to assess poverty in upper-middle-income countries.

These are purchasing-power-parity dollars, or PPP dollars. They compare what money buys across countries. They are not dollars converted at the rupee exchange rate. The $3 line is based on national poverty lines in low-income countries. Crossing it does not establish adequate nutrition, secure work or protection against a medical bill.

The report's calendar-year estimates are also different from direct survey readings. Its `Country_FGT` series aligns surveys to common years using national-accounts growth. India's latest direct survey is the Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES) 2023-24, recorded as 2023 in the Poverty and Inequality Platform (PIP). It puts poverty at 2.57% below $3, 16.12% below $4.20 and 79.98% below $8.30. The report's lined-up estimates for 2024 are 2.29%, 14.88% and 78.78%, respectively. The 2026 and 2030 figures extend those distributions forward; they are estimates, not new household interviews.

At the extreme line, the historical change is large. Lined-up poverty fell from 49.38% in 1990 to 28.51% in 2011 and 2.29% in 2024, when about 33 million people remained below $3 a day. The 2026 nowcast is 1.40%. At the $4.20 lower-middle-income line, the corresponding rates were 77.29%, 59.39% and 14.88%, leaving about 216 million people below the line in 2024. At $8.30, the decline was much slower: from 97.08% in 1990 to 93.08% in 2011 and 78.78% in 2024, or about 1.14 billion people.

The space between those lines contains most of the country. The report's 1,000-bin distribution puts **76.5% of Indians between $3 and $8.30 a day in 2024**. Reduce every bin's real consumption by a hypothetical 10%, then count again. The share below $4.20 rises from 14.9% to 23.2%; at $3 it rises from 2.3% to 4.4%, and at $8.30 from 78.8% to 85.1%. This static exercise shows the thin buffer above each cutoff. It does not predict a shock or track which families will fall back into poverty. Both endpoints use the same bins, which approximate the national lineup and resolve to about 0.1 percentage point.

The poverty gap adds depth to that count. Divide the population-average shortfall by the share below the line, and the result is the average shortfall among poor people. In the direct HCES 2023-24 PIP estimates, consumption among those below $3 averages about 13.2% short of that line. At $4.20 the shortfall is 15.9%; at $8.30 it is 34.1%. The higher line captures a much deeper average deficit, not merely more households just below a cutoff. These ratios are approximate because the API inputs are rounded.

A separate measure lets the poverty line rise with domestic living standards. Under the World Bank's 2021 PPP method, the Societal Poverty Line is `max($3.00, $1.30 + 0.5 × national median daily consumption)`. For India, the line was $3.23 in 2011-12 and $4.27 in 2023-24. The share below it fell from 33.44% to 17.35%. The hero chart shows this survey-year series alongside the three calendar-year lines, so its dates and estimation basis differ.

The **Prosperity Gap** answers another question. For each person, divide the $28-a-day prosperity standard by their daily consumption, then average those ratios across everyone. Poorer people contribute more to the score because their ratios are larger. This is not $28 divided by average consumption, and a national score of five does not mean multiplying everyone's spending by five would bring everyone to $28.

India's survey-based Prosperity Gap fell from 9.70 in 1993 to 7.67 in 2011, 5.36 in 2022 and 4.95 in 2023. The state map shows how unevenly that shortfall is distributed. Using the Global Subnational Atlas of Poverty's 2023 reference year, Jharkhand's score is 6.72, Chhattisgarh's 6.31 and Odisha's 5.92. Kerala is at 3.91, Goa at 3.16 and Chandigarh at 2.22. These are population averages of individual ratios, not uniform growth targets for each state's residents.

Young children face another disadvantage within those states. In the report's separate Subnational Poverty and Inequality Database (SPID), the share of children aged 0-5 living in households below $8.30 is higher than the all-age share in every state and union territory. In Kerala, the comparison is 75.8% against 58.5%; in Maharashtra, 91.0% against 82.0%. The chart shows selected states. These groups overlap: the all-age population includes the children. The measure describes the household consumption available per person, not what a child eats or receives within the home.

## How did a 2021 PPP price revision and a new survey questionnaire rewrite the numbers?

A poverty estimate depends on what households report, how that spending is valued and where the line is set. India's recent estimates changed on all these fronts. The disputes are easier to follow when those changes are kept separate.

### The PPP revision changed the yardstick

The World Bank adopted the 2021 PPP poverty lines in June 2025. The old lines of $2.15, $3.65 and $6.85 in 2017 PPP became $3, $4.20 and $8.30 in 2021 PPP. Comparing the two PPP vintages within the frozen September 2026 PIP release holds the release and survey year fixed.

For HCES 2023-24, the extreme-poverty estimate changes from 1.02% at the old $2.15 line to 2.57% at the new $3 line. At the lower-middle-income benchmark, the estimate goes the other way, from 19.91% at $3.65 to 16.12% at $4.20. At the upper-middle-income benchmark, it moves from 79.81% to 79.98%.

The dollar amounts belong to different price bases. Their percentage changes alone do not tell us how much stricter each line became in India. PPPs, domestic price adjustments and the underlying national poverty lines all matter. These revisions change the measuring rule; they do not describe households suddenly gaining or losing consumption.

PIP uses one national consumption PPP factor for India, Rs 19.47 per international dollar in 2021 prices. The frozen national series does not contain separate rural and urban PPP conversion factors. Rural and urban comparisons require attention to the spatial price adjustments in the welfare aggregate, rather than invented sector-specific PPPs.

### The survey changed too

HCES 2022-23 and 2023-24 collect the food, consumables and services, and durables modules over three monthly visits, rather than the earlier single-visit schedule. Longer item lists and changes in fieldwork can change how much spending households recall. A cleaner questionnaire is valuable, but it complicates comparison with an older survey.

Himanshu, Peter Lanjouw and Philipp Schirmer examine that break in Tinbergen Institute Discussion Paper TI 2025-069/V. They use survey-to-survey imputation to estimate consumption on a more consistent historical basis. Their models do not measure an alternative truth directly; they depend on the stability of relationships between consumption and the characteristics used to predict it.

The chart deliberately shows several poverty lines as well as several survey treatments. Direct HCES yields 2.35% at the old $2.15 line and 5.25% at the new $3 line. Applying the Rangarajan line directly gives 7.2%. The paper's CES-based Tendulkar estimates range from 11.3% to 13.6%; its EUS-to-PLFS Tendulkar estimates include 17.5%, 18.3% and 19.9%. Under the Rangarajan-equivalent MMRP line, the sector-wide EUS-to-PLFS model gives 25.9%.

That spread cannot be attributed entirely to the questionnaire. It combines different poverty lines, recall concepts and models. The useful comparisons hold the line fixed while changing the survey treatment. Standard errors and model assumptions matter too; this chart is not a confidence interval for India's poverty rate.

### Free goods count as consumption, with limits

MoSPI publishes HCES spending with and without the imputed value of specified goods received free through welfare programmes. Foodgrain and items such as uniforms or bicycles have consumption value even when the household pays nothing for them. This is distinct from a cash transfer or market earnings.

In the official HCES 2023-24 press note, average monthly per-capita spending rises from **Rs 4,122 to Rs 4,247 in rural India** when these items are included, a Rs 125 increase, or about 3.0%. In urban India it rises from Rs 6,996 to Rs 7,078, a Rs 82 increase, or about 1.2%. Those are sector averages; they cannot establish the boost for the poorest households or how much poverty would change without transfers.

The boundary matters. MoSPI does not impute the value of free health services or waived education fees in these estimates, because their value is difficult to establish through household recall. Consumption with welfare imputation therefore still excludes some public services. Nor is MoSPI's published with-imputation distribution identical to the World Bank's harmonised welfare aggregate, which makes its own adjustments for international poverty measurement.

### The survey and National Accounts tell different growth stories

In PIP, India's consumption Gini falls from 28.78 in 2011-12 to 25.51 in 2022-23 and 23.00 in 2023-24. The richest survey decile's share of consumption falls from 24.80% to 20.52% over the same endpoints. These describe measured consumption, not the distribution of income or wealth.

The two latest HCES rounds offer a cleaner comparison because PIP explicitly marks 2022-23 and 2023-24 as comparable. Average real consumption grows 13.1% for the poorest surveyed tenth, against a measured decline of 2.9% for the richest surveyed tenth. The national mean rises 4.7%. This helps explain the falling consumption Gini within these rounds. Each decile contains different people at each date, however, and the survey's coverage of affluent households still matters. The result cannot establish that rich Indians as a whole became poorer.

Index the available series to 1977 and the divergence becomes clear. Real GDP per capita grows 5.70-fold by 2023, National Accounts household consumption per capita grows 4.49-fold, and survey mean consumption grows 2.09-fold. Each line is indexed from its own starting value; their original currency units are not placed on one scale.

This discrepancy warrants scrutiny. It does not, by itself, prove that missing rich households explain the whole gap or that National Accounts supply the correct distribution. Coverage, valuation, reporting errors and survey design can all contribute. The chart cannot tell us how to allocate the missing consumption across households. [Consumption inequality](/articles/income-inequality/) is only one part of the larger inequality question.

## Why do working Indians stay poor even when they work 44 hours a week?

The World Bank's jobs chapter makes a useful distinction between having work and earning enough from it. Its tables also use different poverty thresholds. The working-hours comparison uses $3 a day; most of the employment and education comparisons use $8.30. Mixing them would change who counts as poor halfway through an argument.

Among paid employees in households below $3 a day, average weekly hours are **44.05**, against 44.95 above the line. Among self-employed and unpaid workers, the corresponding figures are 37.05 and 40.61 hours. These are averages among people already working, not among all adults. Hours alone clearly cannot account for the difference in household consumption.

At the $8.30 line, the share of workers in poor households without secondary education falls from 86.71% in 2003 to 56.47% in 2024. Yet the share of working-age adults employed in those households falls from 60.72% to 58.29%. Among non-poor households, employment rises from 50.25% to 57.58%. These comparisons do not track the same families over time: households can move between poverty groups. They also do not isolate the effects of schooling, women's participation or age composition.

Job structure differs sharply. In 2024, 58.24% of workers in households below $8.30 are self-employed, against 45.24% above the line. Agriculture employs 45.92% of poor workers and 24.77% of non-poor workers. These descriptive differences help explain why [the quality of employment](/articles/how-many-good-jobs-is-india-creating/) matters alongside the employment rate. They do not establish the causal return from moving a particular worker into another job.

The report also supplies an accounting counterfactual. In its combined employment-and-worker-earnings scenario, India's $8.30 poverty rate falls from 78.78% to 56.61%, a **22.17 percentage-point reduction**. The poverty gap at that line, which averages the shortfall across the whole population as a share of the line, falls from 25.73% to 16.64%. At $3, the simulated headcount falls from 2.28% to 1.14%. The tiny difference between this baseline and the chapter-one 2.29% figure comes from separate replication outputs; we keep each as supplied.

That is a large simulated change, but it still leaves more than half the population below $8.30. It is not a forecast, a costed programme or proof that one particular jobs policy would deliver those gains. The processed counterfactual file gives combined results; it does not identify India's education-versus-sector contribution to the 22.17-point reduction.

## What questions about Indian poverty can still not be answered?

Money and capability do not move in lockstep. In the World Bank's multidimensional measure for HCES 2022-23, 17.74% of Indians are poor, against 5.25% below the monetary extreme line in the same survey. Sanitation deprivation affects 29.60%, lack of an adult with completed primary schooling 13.82%, drinking-water deprivation 6.39% and electricity deprivation 1.02%. These overlapping deprivations cannot be added together.

Nutrition provides a different lens, with a different denominator and date. The fifth National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5), conducted in 2019-21, found 35.5% of children under five stunted and 32.1% underweight. These are not percentages of all Indians or new findings from HCES 2023-24. The World Bank's multidimensional measure excludes nutrition and health, so it cannot stand in for them either.

Medical spending exposes another limit of expenditure measures. A household can finance treatment by borrowing or selling assets; the expenditure records consumption while the financing damages its balance sheet. The World Bank's financial-protection series for **2022** reports 30.94% facing financial hardship from out-of-pocket health spending, including 22.27% facing impoverishing spending. These are separate health-financing indicators, not the percentage pushed below this article's $3 line by one hospital bill.

HCES also samples households anew rather than following the same families year after year. It cannot measure how often a particular family falls back into poverty after a crop failure or an illness. Dividing household consumption by household size also leaves the allocation of food and healthcare within the home out of view.

The report's planet chapter adds environmental exposure, but its supplied exposure workbooks give regional and income-group aggregates. They cannot support an India state-level heat-or-flood poverty overlay. Presenting South Asia's exposure as India's would create a geographical error. That is a reporting gap to fill with compatible local hazard and household data.

The international comparison becomes less flattering at a higher line. In the common 2024 estimates, 78.8% of Indians are below $8.30 a day, against 68.5% in Bangladesh, 68.1% in Indonesia and 18.2% in Viet Nam; Pakistan is at 87.2%. India has a lower $3 poverty share than Bangladesh and Indonesia, but a higher share below $8.30. These are selected Asian comparators, not one income group. Indonesia's observation is marked actual; the other four are projections or lineup estimates, so the common year is not a common survey date.

India's role in global poverty changes with the benchmark too. In the chapter-one 2024 tables, India accounts for 4.03% of the world's population below $3, 14.56% below $4.20 and 30.42% below $8.30. In the separate 2026 table, it contributes 18.49% of the Global Prosperity Gap, against 18.31% of world population. The latter compares average welfare ratios, not a count of people below $28. A small share of global extreme poverty and a large share of the world's wider consumption shortfall can coexist.

## How to read these numbers: methodology and caveats

The frozen sources are the World Bank's 2026 report reproducibility package, FR_WLD_2026_763, the September 2026 PIP releases in both PPP vintages, MoSPI's HCES 2023-24 press note, the Tinbergen paper, NFHS-5 and the World Bank's 2022 health-financing observations. The package contains four chapters. Its processed tables can be audited without treating them as household-level raw microdata.

The three national trajectories use `Country_FGT`, with all annual observations from 1990 to 2030. Estimates after 2024 are drawn dashed. The societal-poverty series uses direct survey-year PIP observations, not the calendar-year lineup. Binned consumption values are approximate locations in the distribution; bin-based shares resolve only to a tenth of a percentage point.

The state figures use the `Data 2023` sheet of `AM26_GSAP_data.xlsx`. They are tied to HCES 2023-24 and the atlas's 2023 reference year. The `Data 2024` sheet applies a later lineup and should not be substituted without relabelling. State poverty shares are multiplied by 100 to convert proportions to percentages. Prosperity Gap values remain ratios.

The child comparison stays entirely within SPID's direct HCES survey estimates, labelled year 2023 and reference year 2023.58 in the workbook. The same-state rows use age groups `0-5` and `ALL`; the latter includes the former. Those estimates are distinct from the GSAP calendar lineup used for the state map and must not be substituted for it. No national child headcount is inferred from state percentages without compatible population weights.

The decile-growth calculation multiplies each national mean by that decile's consumption share and divides by 0.1, then computes the percentage change between the two comparable rounds. The consumption-loss exercise multiplies every 2024 bin's welfare by 0.9, keeps its population weight fixed and recomputes the share below each line. It assumes an equal proportional loss, and represents neither a forecast nor the effect of 10% price inflation. The Asian comparison uses `Country_FGT` at all three common lines in 2024, retaining each country's actual/projection flag.

For a rough rupee illustration, PIP's national 2021 PPP factor of Rs 19.47 multiplied by its unrounded 2023 consumer-price ratio gives about Rs 22.55 per PPP dollar in survey-year prices. Using 365/12 days per month, $3 is roughly Rs 68 a day or Rs 2,060 a month; $4.20 is Rs 95 a day or Rs 2,880 a month; $8.30 is Rs 187 a day or Rs 5,700 a month. These are national price equivalents, not separate rural and urban budgets or recommended spending minima.

The welfare-imputation chart uses only published sector averages. Its boost is `(with imputation − without imputation) / without imputation × 100`. A previous reconstruction by consumption fractile could not be independently reproduced from the frozen story files and has been removed. The current comparison makes no claim about who receives the transfer or its effect on the poverty headcount.

The jobs chapter uses $3 for weekly hours and $8.30 for the employment, schooling and job-structure splits. Its simulation is an accounting exercise. Changes in group averages do not follow the same households, and the charted associations do not identify policy effects. Every numerical artifact is reconciled separately to its source cells; this does not remove the survey and modelling uncertainties described above.
