## How much of India did foreigners actually pay for?

About 3.1 per cent of it last year.

While the $38.9 billion in foreign direct investment that India received in 2025 is frequently quoted, that absolute dollar amount means little on its own for a $4.1 trillion economy like India. This inflow was up 44 per cent from 2024, ranking India twelfth in the world. Although typical reports focus solely on this absolute level, UNCTAD publishes the share of gross fixed capital formation, which represents the money spent on factories, machines, roads and buildings.

Even after India's historic economic reforms of 1991, the share of capital formation financed by foreign money remained low, taking until 1994 to exceed 1 per cent. The share of India's capital formation financed by foreigners finally crossed 5 per cent in 2006, peaking at 10.3 per cent in 2008 as global capital surged. By 2010, India's share had fallen back to 4.6 per cent. India's share rose again to 8.3 per cent in 2020, before dropping to 4.5 per cent in 2021 and bottoming at 2.2 per cent in 2024. A rebound in 2025 brought the ratio to 3.1 per cent, though this is less than half of the 6.8 per cent achieved in 2015.

These numbers are compiled on the UNCTAD calendar-year net balance-of-payments basis, representing equity, reinvested earnings, and intra-company loans, net of divestment and repatriation. This makes them look smaller than the gross fiscal-year equity inflows reported by India's DPIIT, which are the larger figures more frequently quoted in Indian newspapers.

## Where does India sit among the developing regions, over fifty-six years?

India sits almost always at the bottom.

Across the fifty-six years of World Bank records, India has never once taken in a larger share of its GDP as foreign direct investment than Latin America and the Caribbean, which began at 0.88 per cent in 1970 and stood at 2.68 per cent in 2025. India exceeded Sub-Saharan Africa in only five of those years, and surpassed East Asia and the Pacific in exactly three: 2008, 2009, and 2020, each a year when everyone else was falling faster.

During the closed decades of the 1970s and 1980s, India’s foreign inflows never once exceeded 0.09 per cent of GDP. In 1975, 1976, and 1977, the net balance was negative, reaching a low of -0.03 per cent of GDP in 1977 as more foreign capital left the country than entered. These net outflows coincided with the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act forcing multinationals to dilute their equity, prompting IBM and Coca-Cola to leave India rather than comply.

Even after the economy opened, the peak of India’s foreign inflows reached only 3.62 per cent of GDP in 2008, compared to a global peak of 5.27 per cent in 2007. By 2025, India’s inflows stood at 0.99 per cent of GDP, remaining below the global average of 1.26 per cent and the East Asia and Pacific average of 1.27 per cent.

## Did India climb at all?

Yes, India climbed from forty-third in the world in 1990 to twelfth in 2025. At the start of the 1990s, the country was a minor destination, receiving just $237 million in 1990 and placing behind Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Colombia and the Philippines. As liberalisation took hold, the country reached thirty-first in 1995, advanced to twenty-seventh in 2001, and broke into the top twenty to hit nineteenth in 2006. The momentum carried India to ninth in 2008 with $47.1 billion, and eventually to its highest ever position of seventh in 2020 with $64.1 billion, placing just behind Germany and ahead of the United Kingdom, Brazil, Mexico and Canada. In 2025, inflows reached $38.9 billion, up 44 per cent on 2024, after falling to sixteenth in 2023.

But global ranks can flatter India when investment collapses elsewhere. India's presence at ninth in 2008 and 2009 reflects the global financial crisis, which reduced inflows to rich nations more severely. Similarly, during the 2020 collapse in global investment, major telecom stake sales kept Indian inflows high while other destinations suffered. When other economies recovered, India fell back to fifteenth in 2024.

Even so, the climb from India's lowest rank of seventy-second in 1991 to twelfth in 2025 is a real achievement. A country that was nearly invisible to multinational boards in 1990 is now a routine member of the top fifteen.

## Is there more than one way to build a country?

Two paths exist, and India is in the domestic-capital camp with Korea, not the foreign-led camp with Viet Nam.

In the foreign-led model, which built modern Viet Nam and Poland, multinational firms bring the factories, machine tools, and export networks directly. Viet Nam has not fallen below 13.8 per cent of its capital formation in any year since 2011, and reached 14.7 per cent in 2025. Averaged over the 2011 to 2025 period, foreign investment paid for 15.0 per cent of capital formation in Viet Nam, 15.4 per cent in Poland, and 13.3 per cent in Malaysia.

China, Korea, and India represent the alternative path, relying on domestic savings to finance building while purchasing technology separately. Between 2011 and 2025, foreign capital financed 2.5 per cent of capital formation in China, 2.6 per cent in Korea, and 4.9 per cent in India.

Korea reached this low share through deliberate strategy, restricting foreign ownership for decades, directing credit to its chaebols, and licensing technology rather than letting it arrive with an owner attached. India ended up in the same self-financing camp without ever deciding to be there, spending three decades actively trying to raise the share only to see it remain on the margins.

The live question is whether the terms of global competition have now shifted against India. Strategic manufacturing has moved to heavily subsidised sectors, meaning India must now compete against deep-pocketed rivals in an era of direct subsidies.

## Is India unusual in its own neighbourhood?

No, India is not a South Asian anomaly.

Across South Asia, foreign investment is a minor player in what countries actually build. Between 2011 and 2025, foreign direct investment financed an average of just 4.9 per cent of India's capital formation. Across the same fifteen years, the ratio was 4.6 per cent in Pakistan and 3.9 per cent in Sri Lanka, while Bangladesh averaged just 2.2 per cent. These numbers show that a low reliance on foreign money is a structural regional pattern, not a failure unique to New Delhi.

In fact, Pakistan outpaced India on this measure in 23 of the last 36 years, including each of the three years to 2025, while Sri Lanka did so in 22 years, including each of the last four. But this does not mean Islamabad or Colombo are out-competing India for multinational factories. Both Pakistan and Sri Lanka have much smaller capital formation, and recent balance-of-payments crises further depressed their domestic spending on infrastructure and machinery. In 2024, for instance, Pakistan's share of 6.2 per cent was generated by just $2.7 billion of foreign investment, while India's share of 2.2 per cent represented $27.1 billion. Bangladesh exhibits an even lower reliance, with foreign investment financing just 1.34 per cent of its capital formation in 2025, down from a peak of 6.74 per cent in 1997.

## What does all of it add up to, per Indian?

About $547.

That is the cumulative foreign direct investment India received per person over the thirty-six years between 1990 and 2025. Dividing a thirty-six year flow of $800 billion by the 2025 population of 1,464 million provides a scale intuition rather than an arithmetically precise ratio, but it shows how thin the paint is spread. In Noida or Chennai, that sum would barely cover a budget smartphone or a few months of room rent.

In absolute terms, the $800 billion India received over this period ranks 16th in the world. Of course, Singapore and Ireland are conduit economies as much as destinations, booking transit flows that yield per-person figures of $292,344 and $132,690 respectively.

Even when excluding financial hubs, India sits near the bottom of the global ladder. China took in $2,243 per person on a cumulative inflow of $3,177 billion. Viet Nam received $2,652 per person and Thailand reached $3,319. Malaysia, with a population of 36 million, accumulated $7,127 per person from a cumulative inflow of $256 billion. Even Brazil and Mexico, at $6,444 and $6,554 respectively, operate in a different tier.

Within its own neighbourhood, India is not even the leader. Sri Lanka received $813 per person over these thirty-six years. Indonesia reached $1,214. While India sits ahead of Nigeria at $485, Pakistan at $227, and Bangladesh at $202, the regional pattern suggests that low foreign capital penetration is a shared South Asian trait.

## When the factories left China, where did they go?

They went to Southeast Asia.

During the two periods of 2013 to 2017 and 2021 to 2025, India's average annual foreign direct investment fell from $38.2 billion to $37.6 billion, a drop of about 2 per cent. Meanwhile, UNCTAD's developing Southeast Asia aggregate went from $126 billion to $219 billion over the same comparison, a rise of 73 per cent. Even China's inflows rose from $132 billion to $151 billion, though its share of developing-economy inflows fell from 23.7 per cent in 2020 to 11.6 per cent in 2025.

In 2015, Southeast Asia received 2.6 times as much FDI as India. By 2025, it received 6.3 times as much. Southeast Asia's share of developing-country inflows rose from 15.5 per cent in 2015 to 27.1 per cent in 2025, while India's fell from 6.0 per cent to 4.3 per cent.

The data shows where the money went, not why. Land costs, labour law, tariff policy, logistics, the pace of clearances, and the depth of component supply chains are all live candidates, but this dataset arbitrates none of them. Also, UNCTAD's Southeast Asia aggregate includes Singapore, a large share of whose inflows are booked rather than built there, so the level is flattered even though the trend is not.

## Is India gaining on its actual competitors?

No, India has lost ground to its competitors in Southeast Asia over the last ten years.

In 1990 India took in just 0.7 per cent of all direct investment flowing to developing economies. That share fell to a minimum of 0.2 per cent in 1991, before rising to 5.97 per cent in 2015. By 2020 the Indian share peaked at 10.18 per cent, though this spike was primarily because corporate investment elsewhere collapsed during the pandemic while domestic telecom companies closed several multibillion-dollar fundraising rounds. Since the 2020 peak, India's trajectory has reversed. In 2025 India's share of these inflows fell back to 4.32 per cent.

Over that same ten-year period, Southeast Asia's share of developing-world inflows climbed from 15.5 per cent in 2015 to 27.1 per cent in 2025. This divergence between India and Southeast Asia reveals a competitive challenge. While India's global rank can look stable because large, volatile flows between wealthy Western nations dominate the worldwide totals, India does not compete with Germany or the United Kingdom for manufacturing plants. The country competes directly with Viet Nam, Indonesia, Malaysia and Mexico. Against this specific peer group, India has been losing share for a decade.

Excluding offshore tax havens from the developing-world aggregate isolates the real, physical factories built in India or Viet Nam rather than paper transactions. Looking at this real competitive arena shows that the gains from supply-chain diversification have bypassed India in favour of its neighbours.

## How much of the economy do foreigners own?

About 13.5 per cent of it, by the latest count.

Annual flows are noisy, but the accumulated stock of investment represents the slow, undeniable verdict on three decades of Indian policy. By 2025, that accumulated foreign ownership in India stood at 13.5 per cent of GDP. In this ten-country comparison, only Bangladesh, at 4.3 per cent, sits lower. China is at 19.3 per cent, Indonesia is at 23.8 per cent, and Poland is at 40.6 per cent. In Latin America, Mexico's stock is 44.5 per cent of its output and Brazil's is 50.7 per cent. The manufacturing hubs of Southeast Asia sit in another league: Viet Nam has reached 55.1 per cent, Malaysia 56.8 per cent, and Thailand 66.5 per cent.

These are ownership facts, and they carry the exact political choice India debated for forty years. A low share means eighty-six per cent of India's productive assets are owned by people who live in the country. Thailand, at 66.5 per cent, has made the opposite bet: more than sixty-six per cent of its productive assets are owned abroad, and the profits flow there accordingly. But for India, the number makes one thing clear: foreign capital did not build this economy. Whatever built the remaining eighty-six per cent of Indian output, it was domestic savings.

These stock figures are compiled at book value, the 2025 numbers are preliminary, and UNCTAD revises them frequently.

## Did that money build anything, or just buy it?

Mostly, it changed the name on the share register of existing companies in India.

A greenfield investment adds new factories and machines to India, while an acquisition simply hands ownership of an existing Indian asset to a buyer abroad. In 2018, net foreign acquisitions of Indian companies reached $33.6 billion, dominated by the Walmart acquisition of Flipkart, against $42.2 billion of recorded foreign direct investment. During the investment peak in 2020, foreign acquisitions took up $21.8 billion against $64.1 billion of recorded inflows.

These two lines move on different clocks in India. Because deal values and balance-of-payments flows come from different sources with different timing conventions, acquisitions compiled by UNCTAD are not a clean subset of recorded inflows. But their relative shapes show how foreign interest in India shifts. In 2024, net foreign acquisitions fell to minus $1.4 billion, and in 2025 they fell further to minus $3.3 billion, their lowest point since 1990. This means foreign firms sold more Indian assets than they bought in the very years that produced the 44 per cent rebound in recorded inflows.

What is left is the greenfield pipeline of new projects in India. When foreign firms withdraw from existing acquisitions, the remaining inflows entering India must represent new capacity, even if the transition is slow.

## Why do the announcements not match the arrivals?

Because they are different things, and only one of them is money that moved.

India announced $89.5 billion of greenfield projects in 2023, a record $111.1 billion in 2024, and $74.1 billion in 2025. The foreign investment actually recorded in those three years was $28.1 billion, $27.1 billion and $38.9 billion. The year India announced its largest project pipeline on record was the year it booked its smallest inflow in a decade.

An announced greenfield project is an intention, compiled by UNCTAD from a commercial database of company press releases rather than official balance-of-payments statistics. A plant announced in 2024 may appear as balance-of-payments money spread across 2026, 2027 and 2028, or may be quietly shelved when the numbers stop working. Recorded FDI, by contrast, is the actual cash crossing India's borders in a calendar year. Subtracting the arrivals from the announcements produces a shortfall figure that means nothing, though it is routinely reported in Indian newspapers.

Since 2022, announced projects have run far ahead of actual arrivals, widening a gap that has always existed. When Indian policymakers quote foreign investment figures from a stage, they almost always use the larger announcement totals.

UNCTAD attributes a large part of India's 44 per cent rebound in 2025 to a cumulative $14.5 billion commitment by Google to data centres and internet infrastructure, and a $4 billion hydrogen investment by the Polish firm Hynfra. A recovery resting on just two corporate commitments is thinner than the headline suggests, and data centres do not build the manufacturing supply chains that Indian industrial policy was written to attract.

## Which era actually worked?

The Make in India years, and by a comfortable margin.

Five policy eras divide the years since liberalisation, and they show that the effort to attract foreign capital peaked both in absolute volume and in domestic weight between 2015 and 2020. During those six years, India received $285 billion in inward direct investment. That money financed an average of 6.31 per cent of gross fixed capital formation, the highest share of any era on record. It was also an era of highly concentrated inflows: Indian firms sent just $59 billion abroad, leaving a net inflow of $226 billion.

Compare this to the decades before and after. The opening decade from 1991 to 2000 brought $19 billion, which financed a mere 1.77 per cent of capital formation, while $1 billion went outward, leaving a net of $17 billion. The boom from 2001 to 2008 brought $122 billion at an average share of 4.69 per cent, but saw $63 billion go outward, leaving a net of $59 billion. The subsequent period from 2009 to 2014 received $186 billion, financing 5.14 per cent of capital formation. In contrast, the most recent period from 2021 to 2025 has seen a sharp retreat. Inward flows fell to $188 billion, financing just 3.33 per cent of capital formation, while outward investment by Indian entities surged to $106 billion, leaving a net inflow of only $83 billion.

The peak era of 2015 to 2020 carries two qualifications. First, the totals are not directly comparable because the eras are of different lengths. Second, the period ended in 2020, a year when global direct investment collapsed during the pandemic while India booked several exceptionally large acquisitions in a single telecommunications company. Excluding that single year narrows the gap between the eras. Yet even with the 2020 spike included, the best policy era on record peaked at financing roughly one-sixteenth of what India built. The target was never met, and the domestic-capital model remained the real engine of Indian development.

## Do Indian firms build abroad?

Yes, and occasionally on a scale that approaches what foreign companies plan to build inside India.

In 2022, Indian companies announced a record $42.3 billion of greenfield projects overseas, compared to $65.8 billion announced by foreign firms into India. Even in 2025, as foreign announcements into India stood at $74.1 billion, Indian firms announced $25.3 billion of projects abroad. This outbound drive by Indian firms is not a recent phenomenon. In 2003, when UNCTAD tracking began, Indian companies announced $8.7 billion of greenfield projects abroad, while foreign companies announced $16.2 billion inside India.

These figures represent intentions rather than recorded balance-of-payments flows, showing that Indian capital operates globally. When Indian companies expand, they do not just build in Gujarat or Tamil Nadu. They build in Europe, Africa, North America and Southeast Asia.

In 2008, on the eve of the global financial crisis, Indian corporations announced $26.1 billion of greenfield projects abroad. Even in 2020, the lowest point of the last two decades, outbound announcements by Indian firms came to $7.3 billion. In 2024, when foreign companies announced a record $111.1 billion of projects inside India, Indian firms still announced $17.9 billion abroad.

Outbound projects routed through holding companies can be attributed elsewhere by UNCTAD, and the reverse also happens. Over the last two decades, Indian capital has consistently looked beyond its own borders.

## What does it mean that India now invests abroad too?

It means India is behaving like a maturing economy that has begun to export its own capital.

In 1990, the accumulated stock of foreign direct investment inside India was $1,657 million, which was 13.4 times the $124 million that Indian firms held abroad. By 2010, this gap had closed to 2.1 times, with inward stock at $205,580 million against $96,901 million outward. By 2025, the ratio reached 1.9 times, representing $558,998 million of foreign-owned assets in India against $296,446 million of Indian-owned assets abroad.

This convergence mimics the path of maturing industrial economies like South Korea and Japan, which transitioned from capital importers to major global investors. While South Korea reached this status through deliberate state planning, restricting foreign ownership and directing credit to its chaebols, India ended up in a similar domestic-capital camp without ever deciding to be there. For three decades, Indian policymakers built special economic zones to attract foreign firms, yet it was domestic industrial groups that scaled. Today, this outward stock represents the global expansion of domestic companies such as Tata Motors, Hindalco, ONGC, and Bharti Airtel.

However, not all of this outward investment represents physical factory expansion. A portion consists of corporate redomiciling and holding structures routed through offshore financial hubs. The short-term volatility of these annual flows, including the repatriation and divestment that shapes net figures, is a separate matter of gross-versus-net arithmetic covered in the companion article on the Reserve Bank of India's investment data.

## Where are the Indian multinationals?

India has exactly four.

These are Tata Motors, Hindalco, ONGC and Bharti Airtel, leaving India level with Mexico on the roster of the 100 largest non-financial multinational enterprises from developing economies.

By contrast, China has 41 firms in this developing-economy cohort, with 9 also making the separate world top 100 list. Other Asian economies also outsize India, with Hong Kong counting 9 firms, Singapore and Taiwan Province of China holding 8 each, and Malaysia and Thailand fielding 5 each. On the separate global top 100 list, where India has 0 representatives, Korea has 3.

This missing cohort is the logical counterpart to India's low foreign-investment share, resembling the domestic-capital path of Korea. Korea deliberately restricted foreign ownership for decades, directed domestic credit to its conglomerates, and licensed technology to build national champions like Samsung, Hyundai and LG. India ended up in the same domestic-capital camp without ever deciding to be there, spending three decades trying to attract foreign money while actually building its own industry.

Admittedly, ranking multinationals strictly by their foreign assets undercounts software exporters, which under-represents India's services strength. Yet the gap between 4 firms for India and 41 for China is too large to be a measurement artefact. The lack of domestic giants with deep foreign footprints reflects an economy that has neither fully integrated foreign capital nor successfully projected its own industrial power abroad.

## How to read these numbers: methodology and caveats

Read them as a sober, net record of money that actually stayed in the country. Every figure here uses the UNCTAD net balance-of-payments concept on a calendar-year basis. The net measure includes equity investments and reinvested earnings alongside parent-company loans, after subtracting corporate divestments and repatriations. This is different from the gross figures published by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade. The Indian press usually quotes those gross figures, which are recorded on a fiscal-year basis and do not subtract any outflows. As a result, the numbers in this article will look smaller than those seen elsewhere. Neither compilation is superior, but they measure different transactions.

To verify these findings, India's inflow series was checked line by line against the World Investment Report 2026 annex table 01. It matches exactly. For the long-term view, the World Bank series uses a separate compilation resting on International Monetary Fund balance-of-payments data. The two series agree on India to within 0.12 percentage points of gross domestic product in every year since 1990. This consistency is a reason to trust the estimates, though the two series are never mixed inside a single sentence or chart. Announced greenfield projects are different. They come from a commercial database of corporate announcements rather than official balance-of-payments statistics.

Geographic routing introduces a second limit. A large share of the investment entering India is routed through Mauritius or Singapore, alongside the Netherlands. Source-country attribution represents a routing fact rather than ultimate economic ownership, and a portion of this flow is Indian money returning home under a foreign label. Similarly, outward foreign direct investment does not always represent physical overseas expansion. Some of these transactions reflect holding company structures or corporate redomiciling. To prevent distortions, regional aggregates exclude Caribbean financial centres and special-purpose entities, meaning regional totals will not match simple country additions.

The 2025 figures are preliminary and will be revised in the 2027 World Investment Report. This is a standard feature of balance-of-payments accounting. To illustrate the scale of these revisions, the 2024 inflow for India was recorded as $27.6 billion in last year's report, but was revised to $27.1 billion in this year's edition. Because previous analyses on this site relied on the earlier release, those figures will differ from the updated ones by several hundred million dollars. Both represented the published figures at the time of writing, and neither has been altered to match the other.
