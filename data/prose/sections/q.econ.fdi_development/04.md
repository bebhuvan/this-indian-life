## Is there more than one way to build a country?

Two paths exist, and India is in the domestic-capital camp with Korea, not the foreign-led camp with Viet Nam.

In the foreign-led model, which built modern Viet Nam and Poland, multinational firms bring the factories, machine tools, and export networks directly. Viet Nam has not fallen below 13.8 per cent of its capital formation in any year since 2011, and reached 14.7 per cent in 2025. Averaged over the 2011 to 2025 period, foreign investment paid for 15.0 per cent of capital formation in Viet Nam, 15.4 per cent in Poland, and 13.3 per cent in Malaysia.

China, Korea, and India represent the alternative path, relying on domestic savings to finance building while purchasing technology separately. Between 2011 and 2025, foreign capital financed 2.5 per cent of capital formation in China, 2.6 per cent in Korea, and 4.9 per cent in India.

Korea reached this low share through deliberate strategy, restricting foreign ownership for decades, directing credit to its chaebols, and licensing technology rather than letting it arrive with an owner attached. India ended up in the same self-financing camp without ever deciding to be there, spending three decades actively trying to raise the share only to see it remain on the margins.

The live question is whether the terms of global competition have now shifted against India. Strategic manufacturing has moved to heavily subsidised sectors, meaning India must now compete against deep-pocketed rivals in an era of direct subsidies.
