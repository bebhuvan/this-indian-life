## Which era actually worked?

The Make in India years, and by a comfortable margin.

Five policy eras divide the years since liberalisation, and they show that the effort to attract foreign capital peaked both in absolute volume and in domestic weight between 2015 and 2020. During those six years, India received $285 billion in inward direct investment. That money financed an average of 6.31 per cent of gross fixed capital formation, the highest share of any era on record. It was also an era of highly concentrated inflows: Indian firms sent just $59 billion abroad, leaving a net inflow of $226 billion.

Compare this to the decades before and after. The opening decade from 1991 to 2000 brought $19 billion, which financed a mere 1.77 per cent of capital formation, while $1 billion went outward, leaving a net of $17 billion. The boom from 2001 to 2008 brought $122 billion at an average share of 4.69 per cent, but saw $63 billion go outward, leaving a net of $59 billion. The subsequent period from 2009 to 2014 received $186 billion, financing 5.14 per cent of capital formation. In contrast, the most recent period from 2021 to 2025 has seen a sharp retreat. Inward flows fell to $188 billion, financing just 3.33 per cent of capital formation, while outward investment by Indian entities surged to $106 billion, leaving a net inflow of only $83 billion.

The peak era of 2015 to 2020 carries two qualifications. First, the totals are not directly comparable because the eras are of different lengths. Second, the period ended in 2020, a year when global direct investment collapsed during the pandemic while India booked several exceptionally large acquisitions in a single telecommunications company. Excluding that single year narrows the gap between the eras. Yet even with the 2020 spike included, the best policy era on record peaked at financing roughly one-sixteenth of what India built. The target was never met, and the domestic-capital model remained the real engine of Indian development.
