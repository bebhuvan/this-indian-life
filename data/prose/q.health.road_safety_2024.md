## Who is being killed on India's roads?

The people most often recorded as killed in road crashes are riding two-wheelers. The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (MoRTH) counted 81,780 such deaths in 2024, 46.2 per cent of its national total of 177,175. It also counted 36,526 pedestrians and 4,161 bicyclists. Together, these three groups make up 122,467 deaths, or 69.1 per cent of the police-recorded toll.

The table describes the person who died. It does not identify the person who caused a collision. Nor can its shares tell us the chance of dying on a particular trip. For that, we would need to know how far each group travelled, or at least how many journeys they made. A large death count can reflect frequent travel, danger on each trip, or both. The report's 11,890 deaths in an “Other” victim category are also a reminder that even the classification of the people killed is incomplete.

## What struck pedestrians?

A separate MoRTH table records the *impacting vehicle* in crashes where a pedestrian died. Two-wheelers are the largest named group, at 10,378 of the 36,526 pedestrian deaths. Cars, taxis, vans and light motor vehicles follow with 9,302; trucks and lorries with 5,165. Another 7,857 sit in an “Others” group that cannot be unpacked from this national table.

This is a useful second view of the same 36,526 deaths. A pedestrian appears as the victim in one table and the vehicle recorded in the collision appears in another. Neither table is a court finding about fault. Nor does a count of pedestrians struck by two-wheelers show whether one type of vehicle is more dangerous per kilometre travelled: the number of vehicles and their travel differ. The important practical question is how conflicts between people walking and moving vehicles occur in specific places. These national counts tell us which collisions to investigate; they cannot reconstruct the street, speed or conduct in each case.

## How old were the people who died?

The 18–25, 25–35 and 35–45 age bands contain 117,026 deaths between them, 66.1 per cent of the total. The report records 10,117 deaths among people younger than 18. It could not identify the age of 4,705 people, so that category stays in the chart. Quietly dropping unknowns would make the other shares look more certain than they are.

The age distribution conveys the human reach of the loss, including many people in the years when they may be studying, working or caring for family. It is still a distribution of *deaths*, not a comparison of risk between age groups. Population sizes, travel habits and road use differ. A child in the table might have been a passenger, a pedestrian or a cyclist. We cannot multiply an age share by a road-user share to estimate their overlap. That would assume information the separate national summaries do not supply.

## Is the recorded toll falling?

No. MoRTH reports 177,175 deaths in 2024, up from 172,890 in 2023. That is 4,285 more, an increase of 2.5 per cent after rounding. Police also recorded 487,707 accidents and 471,441 injured people in 2024. An accident is a crash event; a death is a person, and one event can kill more than one person.

The five-year line begins at 138,383 deaths in 2020. Traffic was disrupted by Covid restrictions then, so the rise from that base cannot be read as a clean measure of worsening safety. Even the recent 2023–24 comparison is a count, not a death rate per journey. More traffic, longer travel, more severe crashes and changes in reporting could each move the annual total. The report does not provide the exposure series needed to separate them. The firm observation is that the police-recorded death count rose again.

## Is this mainly an urban toll?

The police record says otherwise. Rural areas account for 125,422 deaths in 2024, 70.8 per cent of the national count. Urban areas account for 51,753. The rural share of recorded accidents is lower, at 62.4 per cent. That gap is consistent with more deaths per *reported accident* in the rural classification, but it does not by itself tell us why. Speed, distance to care, the types of road and vehicle, and reporting differences all need separate evidence.

MoRTH puts an old 2011 rural population share beside its 2024 crash totals. We should not turn that into a current rural-versus-urban death rate. Population is not road travel, the census year is different, and trips cross area boundaries. The rural and urban bars show where the recorded loss was assigned. They do not show the risk faced by a rural resident or the safety of a comparable journey.

## Which kinds of roads carry the toll?

National highways account for 64,772 deaths and state highways for 39,277. Together that is 104,049 people, or 58.7 per cent of the 2024 total. Other roads account for 73,126. That broad residual spans many settings, including local roads whose traffic conditions can differ markedly from one another.

MoRTH notes that national highways were 2.14 per cent of road length as of March 2022. Placing that beside their 36.6 per cent share of 2024 deaths suggests a question, but it cannot calculate the danger of using a highway. A busy highway kilometre carries more vehicle travel than a quiet local road kilometre. The road-length measure is also older than the death count. We need deaths per distance driven, or per comparable journey, before ranking road types by risk. These figures locate the recorded deaths; they do not tell us which road design or behaviour caused them.

## Which states account for the largest totals?

Uttar Pradesh records 24,118 road deaths, the most in 2024. Tamil Nadu follows with 18,449, Maharashtra with 15,715, Madhya Pradesh with 14,791 and Karnataka with 12,390. These five states together record 85,463 deaths, 48.2 per cent of the national total. The chart deliberately shows a selected top five; the remaining deaths are spread across other states and union territories.

A state count is useful for understanding the size of the administrative burden and where closer local reporting would matter. It is a poor safety league table. States differ in population, vehicles, distance travelled, road networks, health care and completeness of police returns. Dividing by population alone would still leave major differences in travel exposure. The 2024 national publication also notes a specific recast of West Bengal data using electronic accident-reporting aggregates. That is another reason to inspect reporting practice before treating a difference between states as a difference in driving behaviour.

## How were fatal crashes classified?

The collision table assigns 37,404 deaths to crashes recorded as a vehicle being hit from behind, 34,030 to hit-and-run cases and 28,400 to head-on collisions. These are large groups, but their names do different jobs. “Hit from back” and “head-on” describe the collision pattern. “Hit and run” describes what happened after a collision. The published categories add to the national total, but they should not be read as a neat list of mutually comparable causes.

The hit-and-run count rose from 31,209 in 2023 to 34,030 in 2024. That change is in the police classification; the table does not tell us whether the underlying behaviour changed, whether identification changed, or both. A rear impact likewise does not identify which driver made an error. Speed, road layout, visibility and enforcement may all matter, but the national row cannot quantify any single explanation. This chart is a map of recorded circumstances, a starting point for investigation rather than a causal verdict.

## What does the report say about helmets and seatbelts?

MoRTH records 54,122 people killed while not wearing a helmet in 2024: 39,470 drivers and 14,652 passengers. It records 14,466 deaths involving non-use of a seatbelt, split between 7,489 drivers and 6,977 passengers. These are large, concrete counts of what police reports say about protective-device use among victims.

They are not estimates of the number of deaths a helmet or seatbelt would certainly have prevented. The counterfactual for each crash is unknown. The table also does not give us a consistent denominator for all riders or occupants who used a device, and the helmet and belt groups refer to different vehicle situations. It would be wrong to divide either count by all road deaths and call that the risk of not wearing protection. The evidence points to a serious recorded problem while leaving effectiveness, compliance and crash-specific preventability to other studies.

## When are crashes recorded during the day?

The largest three-hour window is 6 pm to 9 pm: police recorded 102,897 accidents then, 21.1 per cent of the 2024 total. The next largest is 3 pm to 6 pm, with 85,010. Time was unknown for 6,118 accidents, retained in the chart rather than assigned to a convenient hour.

These are **accident counts**, not deaths. The report does not say that 6 pm to 9 pm was the deadliest period for people killed. It also does not measure vehicles on the road by hour. A busy interval could have many crashes even if the chance of a crash per journey were unchanged. Daylight, commuter traffic and road conditions may be relevant, but this national time table cannot divide their effects. It does tell an investigator where to ask for finer local crash, traffic and lighting data.

## Does the annual total hide a monthly pattern?

The monthly death counts move within 2024. November records 15,922 and December 16,007, while July records 13,033 and August 12,959. March and April are also above 15,600. These twelve monthly figures sum to the annual 177,175, so they are another view of the same deaths rather than an additional toll.

It is easy to attach a seasonal story to those differences. A single year is not enough. Months differ in length, traffic volume, festivals, weather and reporting, and the source does not isolate these influences. The Covid-era years in the report make an uncritical five-year monthly average especially misleading. The 2024 profile is useful for showing that a flat annual average conceals variation; it is not evidence that monsoon weather, a particular festival or any one seasonal factor caused a change. A fuller seasonal analysis would require multiple comparable years and matching exposure data.

## How complete is the police record?

The World Health Organization (WHO) offers an independent check for **2021**, not 2024. Its India country profile reports 153,972 police-recorded fatalities that year, matching the MoRTH annual series. WHO's modelled estimate for the same year is 216,618 deaths, with a 95 per cent uncertainty interval from 193,271 to 239,965. Even the lower end exceeds the police count.

The comparison suggests substantial incompleteness in the administrative record. The difference between the two central figures is not a list of identified missing people. WHO's estimate comes from a model and has uncertainty. It cannot be turned into a multiplier for MoRTH's 2024 count. MoRTH itself discusses deaths that may not flow back from hospitals into police records when systems are not linked. It says this national publication was assembled from consolidated police returns, rather than directly from its e-DAR electronic accident database. These limits affect how literally to treat every chart on this page.

## What can these figures say about prevention?

The report is rich enough to direct sharper questions. Why are so many victims on two-wheelers or walking? What happens on the roads where rural deaths are recorded? Why do some police categories contain so many cases? The official tables give scale and location, but they do not establish which intervention would have prevented a particular death. The “impacting vehicle” is not a legal finding; a recorded traffic violation is not a controlled causal study; non-use of a helmet is not proof that every such death was preventable.

Better measurement is part of the response. Linking crash, hospital and death records would clarify how many people die after the initial police report. Counting journeys or kilometres by road user and road type would let us compare risk on comparable exposure. Local records could join age, victim type, vehicle, road design and injury outcome without guessing from separate national margins. Until then, the annual report is a detailed map of police-recorded harm with important blank spaces.

## How to read these numbers: methodology and caveats

The main source is MoRTH's *Road Accidents in India 2024*, compiled from calendar-year state and union-territory police returns. We checked every plotted observation here against rows in the original PDF, and reconciled the published category totals. Tables 1.1, 1.5, 2.1, 3.3, 4.2, 4.4, 4.5, 5.6, 7.1, 7.2 and 7.3 supply the chart data. The complete model-generated transcription of the 260-plus-page report remains a draft; this article's selected cells have their own source audit.

Percentages in the death charts use the 177,175 recorded 2024 deaths as the denominator unless stated otherwise. The time-of-day chart counts *accidents* out of 487,707, not fatalities. The selected top-five state chart is not a complete partition. Published age bands are treated as separate categories despite shared endpoints in print; unknown ages remain unknown. The WHO comparison comes from its [India country profile](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/road-safety-ind-2023-country-profile), released in April 2024, and refers only to 2021. These counts have no matched per-journey or per-kilometre denominator, and none establishes a single cause of a crash or a death.
