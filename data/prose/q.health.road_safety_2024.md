## Who is being killed on India's roads?

The largest group in India's road-death figures is people on two-wheelers. Police returns compiled by the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (MoRTH) record 81,780 two-wheeler users killed in 2024. That is 46.2 per cent of the 177,175 road deaths in its annual report. Pedestrians account for another 36,526, and bicyclists for 4,161. Together, these three groups make up 122,467 deaths, or 69.1 per cent of the recorded toll.

It matters which side of a collision these labels describe. Table 4.4 classifies the **person who died** by road-user category. It does not say which driver caused the crash. A pedestrian death is not, by itself, evidence about the pedestrian's behaviour; a two-wheeler death is not evidence that the person killed was riding or driving unsafely. Those are questions about circumstances and responsibility that this national table cannot settle.

Nor does the distribution tell us which way of travelling is riskiest. This report does not pair deaths with kilometres travelled, journeys made, hours on the road or a reliable count of people using each mode. A group can have a large share of deaths because it is exposed to traffic often, because an encounter is more dangerous, or both. The chart shows where the loss falls. It is not a ranking of risk per trip.

The smaller categories deserve a look too. The report lists 21,988 deaths among people in cars, taxis, vans and light motor vehicles, 9,687 among truck and lorry users, and 5,659 among auto-rickshaw users. Its “Others” category contains 11,890 deaths and mixes several kinds of road user. It should not be treated as one coherent mode. This is a useful map of the recorded victims, with a visible patch of uncertainty inside the classification itself.

## Is the recorded toll falling?

No. MoRTH reports 177,175 road deaths in 2024, compared with 172,890 in 2023. That is 4,285 more people, a rise of 2.5 per cent after rounding. Police also recorded 487,707 accidents and 471,441 injured people in 2024. An accident and a death are different units: one fatal crash can kill more than one person.

The five-year line starts with 138,383 recorded deaths in 2020 and climbs through 2021, 2022 and 2023. The 2020 starting point needs care. Covid restrictions changed how much traffic was on the road, so comparing 2024 with 2020 cannot isolate a change in safety. The 2023-to-2024 rise is a cleaner recent comparison, though even that is a comparison of counts. It does not tell us how risk changed for someone taking a particular journey.

More vehicles, more trips, longer trips, faster roads, better reporting and changing crash severity could each move the count. This report does not give us the exposure data needed to divide their contributions. The important observed fact is narrower and firmer: the number of deaths in the police record rose again in 2024.

## How old were the people who died?

About two-thirds of the recorded victims were aged 18 to 45. The report's three age bands from 18–25 through 35–45 sum to 117,026 deaths, or 66.1 per cent of the 2024 total. Another 10,117 were younger than 18. The age of 4,705 people was recorded as unknown, and that group stays in the chart rather than being spread across the known ages.

This is a distribution of deaths, not a measure of each age group's chance of dying on the road. There are more people in some age bands than others, and their travel patterns differ. Without age-specific exposure, the bars cannot tell us whether a person in one band faced a higher risk on a comparable trip. The labels also describe the victim, not a driver at fault. A child in this table may have been a passenger, pedestrian or road user of another kind.

The age and mode tables cannot be crossed simply by reading them side by side. We know how many victims were aged 18–45 and how many were two-wheeler users. These national summary tables do not say how many belonged to both groups. Adding the two shares would count overlapping people twice; multiplying them would assume a relationship we have not measured.

## Where are the deaths recorded?

National highways account for 64,772 deaths in 2024 and state highways for 39,277. Together that is 104,049 people, or 58.7 per cent of reported road deaths. Other roads account for 73,126. The “other” label covers very different settings, from district and rural roads to urban and project roads, so even this three-way split hides a lot of variation.

MoRTH notes that national highways made up 2.14 per cent of India's road length as of March 2022. It is tempting to put that small length share beside the 36.6 per cent share of 2024 deaths and call national highways the most dangerous roads. That comparison leaves out traffic volume and distance driven. A kilometre carrying heavy long-distance traffic is not comparable with a kilometre of quiet local road. The length figure is also two years older than the death count. The mismatch can identify a place to investigate; it cannot yield a death rate for a journey or establish why the deaths occurred.

The same restraint applies to the report's state rankings. Uttar Pradesh recorded the most deaths and Tamil Nadu the most accidents in 2024, but raw state totals reflect population, travel and reporting as well as safety. This article does not turn those counts into a league table without comparable exposure measures.

## How complete is the police record?

The World Health Organization (WHO) offers a useful check, for a different year. Its India country profile reports **153,972 recorded road fatalities in 2021**, the same number in MoRTH's annual series. WHO's model-based estimate for 2021 is **216,618 deaths**, with a 95 per cent uncertainty interval from **193,271 to 239,965**. The central estimate is about 41 per cent above the reported figure. Even the lower end of the interval is above it.

These are two ways of measuring the same year's burden. The police figure is an administrative count. WHO's figure is a statistical estimate, with uncertainty. Their gap indicates that the official record may miss a substantial part of the mortality burden, but it does not give us a list of 62,646 individually identified missing deaths. Nor can we multiply the 2024 police count by the 2021 ratio and call the result a 2024 WHO estimate. The country profile does not provide one.

MoRTH itself discusses the limits of police reporting. Its methodology chapter points to deaths after a crash that may not reach the police database when hospital and police records are not linked. It also notes missing detail, uneven reporting and the limits of what its standard forms ask. The ministry describes an electronic accident-reporting system, e-DAR, but says the 2024 national publication was compiled from consolidated police reports rather than directly from e-DAR output. West Bengal's 2024 returns required a specific recast using e-DAR aggregates. These details make the count valuable as a consistent official record while also setting a limit on how literally to treat its completeness.

## What can these figures say about prevention?

They can point to people and places that deserve closer investigation. A policy aimed at road safety would be hard to justify if it ignored the 81,780 recorded two-wheeler deaths or the 36,526 pedestrian deaths. The highway toll likewise deserves attention. But none of these aggregate tables identifies the intervention that would have prevented a particular death. Helmet use, speeds, road design, lighting, emergency care and enforcement require their own evidence. The victim category alone cannot adjudicate among them.

There is a measurement task underneath the prevention task. India needs death records that follow people from the crash scene into care. It also needs to count how many journeys or kilometres each group travels. Only then could it divide deaths by travel and test whether a comparable trip became safer. For now, the annual report shows where reported deaths are concentrated and whether the count rose. It cannot tell us exactly how many deaths went unrecorded in 2024.

## How to read these numbers: methodology and caveats

The main figures come from MoRTH's *Road Accidents in India 2024*. Its Transport Research Wing compiles calendar-year returns from state and union-territory police departments. We read the original PDF directly. The published 2024 total is Table 1.1; victim road-user categories are Table 4.4; ages are Table 4.2; and road categories are Table 2.1. The annual trend uses Table 1.1 for 2020–24. Every value plotted in this article was compared with the original PDF row and column. The full report's model-generated transcription has not been certified as a database.

Shares here divide a group's **recorded deaths in 2024** by **177,175 recorded deaths nationwide**. The 69.1 per cent for two-wheeler users, pedestrians and bicyclists adds their three published counts before dividing. The 66.1 per cent for ages 18–45 adds the report's 18–25, 25–35 and 35–45 bands. The 58.7 per cent for highways adds national- and state-highway deaths. Published age bands are treated as the report's separate categories despite their shared endpoints in print. Unknown ages remain unknown. None of these percentages is a per-trip risk rate.

The WHO comparison is from its [India country profile](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/road-safety-ind-2023-country-profile), released in April 2024, and refers to **2021** mortality. Its estimate and uncertainty interval are modelled, not a direct count or a revision to MoRTH's 2024 table. We show it to explain what a police count can miss, not to construct a new 2024 estimate. The sources do not let us attribute a change in deaths to any single cause.
