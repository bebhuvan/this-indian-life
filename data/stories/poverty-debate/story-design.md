# Story design: q.econ.poverty_debate (draft for editor approval, 3 November 2026)

Built from `source-digest.md` (the full report, read 3 November) and the live article
of 2 November. Status: **awaiting editor approval. Write no prose until it is approved.**

## The question and the answer

- **Headline question:** "Has India really ended poverty? What the World Bank's 2026
  report says, and why economists still argue." (Current: "What does the World Bank's
  2026 poverty report actually show about India, and why do economists still
  disagree?" That puts the report before the reader's question.)
- **Big beat (first two sentences):** By the World Bank's lowest yardstick, about 2 in
  every 100 Indians were extremely poor in 2024: fewer than at any time on record,
  while the rest of the world's progress stalled. By the yardstick the World Bank uses
  for countries at India's stage, about 4 in 5 Indians still were, living on less than
  roughly Rs 187 a person a day.
- **Answer type:** qualified. India has nearly escaped extreme poverty; it has not escaped
  poverty.
- **What the reader should not conclude:** that "2%" means poverty is solved, or that
  "80%" means nothing has changed. Both come from the same spending data and are true at once.

## The conversation this article joins

- **The live debate.** One camp says extreme poverty has all but vanished: Bhalla,
  Bhasin and Virmani (2022) put it at 0.8% in 2019 counting free food; NITI Aayog's
  chief executive said under 5% (2024); SBI Research gave 4-5% (2025). The other camp
  says the fall is real but overstated by a new survey design and a low line:
  Himanshu, Lanjouw and Schirmer (2025); Drèze and Somanchi on the CPHS; Das and
  Drèze on stagnant wages. The World Bank sits between them, and its own documents
  disagree: the report's estimate for 2026 is 10.2% below $4.20, and the October India
  brief's is 14.2%. **This round was set off by** the World Bank's 2026 report and
  India brief (October 2026), which are the article's hook.
- **History used.** One paragraph in Part 4, only where it explains today's fight.
  India measured poverty against a calorie-based line from 1979. Tendulkar replaced it
  in 2009. The Rs 32-a-day affidavit of 2011 turned the line into a political liability.
  Rangarajan's higher line of 2014 was never adopted, and the 2016 NITI task force found
  no consensus. That is why India has no official line and why every recent number is
  someone's choice of line. All of this is already sourced and frozen in
  `data/raw/poverty-debate-followup-2026-11/`.
- **Earlier rounds, to source before use:** the 1971 Dandekar-Rath study that set the
  first calorie norm, and the 1999-2000 recall-period dispute (Deaton and Kozel, *The
  Great Indian Poverty Debate*, 2005). Together they show that every survey change has
  started the same fight. They are not used until each is opened and frozen.
- **Where the article stands.** The fall in extreme poverty is real: none of
  the published estimates compared here finds poverty higher in 2022-23 than in
  2011-12, though the withheld 2017-18 survey suggested spending fell for a time. How far it fell is
  genuinely uncertain, because the survey changed. At the lines that suit a country at
  India's stage, most Indians are still poor. **What would change this view:** a new
  survey with a stable design, and an official national line.

## The ladder

### Part 1: Out of the pit

1. **Is it true that India has ended extreme poverty?** Nearly: 2.3% below $3 a day in
   2024 (about 3.3 crore people), down from 49% in 1990. · evidence: Country_FGT · chart:
   *hero*, the three lines 1990-2030, with projections dashed (drop the societal line from the hero).
2. **$3 a day? What is that in rupees, and who decided it?** About Rs 68 a day, Rs 2,060 a
   month, in 2023-24 prices. It is the typical poverty line of the world's poorest
   countries, not a measure of a decent life. The $4.20 line is about Rs 95, and the $8.30 line about Rs 187. ·
   evidence: PIP PPP factor x CPI (methodology) · box: **What is PPP?** · chart: none
   (a small rupee table in the box could work).
3. **Did the whole world do this, or just India?** The world stalled at about 811
   million extreme poor since 2019-20, while India kept falling. India is now 4% of the
   world's extremely poor, and India alone accounts for about 70% of the world's fall in
   poverty among people with no schooling (2013-24). · evidence: digest A (L1003,
   Fig 1.6, L1645) · chart: **new**, India vs world vs Sub-Saharan Africa at $3, 1990-2026.

### Part 2: Not out of the woods

4. **If almost nobody is that poor, why does it feel like most people are still
   struggling?** Because most Indians sit just above the lowest line: 78.8% are below
   $8.30 (Rs 187 a day, about Rs 5,700 a month per person). Average spending per rural person
   was Rs 4,122 a month in 2023-24, below that line (an average, and roughly comparable prices). India holds 30% of all the
   world's people below $8.30, the largest group anywhere, and is the biggest contributor
   to the global Prosperity Gap. · evidence: Country_FGT, HCES press note, digest A
   (Fig 1B.2) and B (L3175) · chart: **new**, India's share of the world's poor at each
   line (4% / 14.6% / 30.4%).
5. **How close to the edge are they?** 76.5% of Indians live between $3 and $8.30. A 10%
   fall in spending would push the share below Rs 95 a day from about 15% to 23%. ·
   chart: *kept*, the 10% shock (retitled in plain words).
6. **Who is still poor, and where?** Rural more than urban (19.3% vs 8.6% at $4.20).
   A belt from Uttar Pradesh to West Bengal holds about 57% of India's poor. Young
   children are more likely to be poor than adults in every state. · chart: *changed*,
   the state map shows the **share below $4.20** instead of the Prosperity Gap ratio,
   which nobody can read without a lesson; *kept*, children vs all ages.
7. **Are our neighbours doing better?** India has less extreme poverty than Bangladesh
   or Indonesia, but more people below $8.30; Viet Nam is far ahead. · chart: *kept*,
   Asian comparison (moved here from the end).

### Part 3: Working, and still poor

8. **Poor Indians work as hard as anyone. So why are they still poor?** Hours are
   almost the same (44 a week for paid employees below and above $3). What differs is
   what the work pays: poor workers are more often self-employed (58% vs 45%) and in
   farming (46% vs 25%). Worldwide, the report finds that higher earnings per worker,
   not more jobs, did almost all the work of reducing poverty in 2003-24. India's real
   rural wages barely grew in the decade to 2023-24 (Das and Drèze), even as about 13
   crore more people, many of them rural women, took up work. · **new chart: the economy grew, pay did not** (2014-15 = 100, break-free).
   Real GDP per person (NAS, 2011-12 prices) against real rural wage rates for men
   (Labour Bureau occupations as reclassified in November 2013; RBI DBIE report 664;
   deflated by CPI-Rural 2012=100) and the real daily factory wage (ASI wages to
   workers per manday; DBIE 155; deflated by CPI-Combined 2012=100). Every deflator is
   on a single base, with no linking factors. Starting in 2014-15 avoids the 2013
   reclassification. Computed 3 November (built in `scripts/stories/poverty-debate/build_poverty_debate.py` from DBIE files frozen in
   `data/raw/poverty-debate-wages-2026-11/`). By 2024-25, GDP per person was 161.5,
   farm labourers 108.6 (0.8% a year, matching Das and Drèze), non-farm labourers 102.2
   and construction workers 98.3. The factory wage was 105.5 by 2023-24 (0.6% a year).
   Rural 2019-20 and 2020-21 lack twelve full months of data (the pandemic) and are
   left blank, not interpolated. An earlier version that linked CPI-IW bases showed a
   false 10% factory jump in 2023-24; the single-base deflator removes it. PLFS
   earnings (2017-18 onward, July-June years) stay in the prose. · charts: *kept*, hours;
   *changed*, the education/employment dumbbell is replaced by **what poor workers do**
   (self-employed, farming), from the chapter 4 files.
9. **What would it take?** In the World Bank's simulation, if poor households had the
   jobs and pay of better-off ones, the share below $8.30 would fall from 79% to 57%.
   That is a large change, and it still leaves most Indians below the line. · chart: *kept*, simulation.

### Part 4: Why nobody agrees on the number

10. **Why doesn't India have its own poverty line any more?** The last official line was
    2011-12 (Tendulkar, 21.9%). Rangarajan's higher line (29.5%) was never adopted, and the Rs 32-a-day
    affidavit of 2011 made the whole exercise politically radioactive. No line has been set since.
    · box: **Tendulkar or Rangarajan: where should the line be?**
11. **Why did the World Bank's numbers jump in 2025?** New price data (2021 PPP) moved
    the lines; extreme poverty in 2023-24 went from 1.0% to 2.6% while the $4.20 rate
    fell from 19.9% to 16.1%. The yardstick changed, not people's lives. · chart: *changed*,
    the PPP chart cut to 2023-24's three lines, with both vintages in the legend.
12. **Did the new survey make poverty look smaller?** The 2022-23 survey asked more
    questions over three visits; spending recall rose. Depending on the line and how you
    bridge the old and new surveys, 2022-23 poverty is anywhere from 2.4% to 26%. Nobody
    finds that poverty rose. The argument is about how far and how fast it fell. ·
    box: **Did the new survey shrink poverty?** (Himanshu-Lanjouw-Schirmer; the
    CPHS removal; NITI and SBI claims; the World Bank's "both genuine gains and better
    measurement") · chart: *kept*, the 2.4%-26% range.
13. **Is India really one of the most equal countries?** No. India measures spending, and
    most countries measure income, which is always more unequal. Within the two comparable new
    surveys, the poorest tenth's spending grew fastest; but the survey has captured a
    shrinking share of the spending the national accounts record since 1977. · box:
    **"Fourth most equal country"?** · charts: *kept*, decile growth; *kept*, national
    accounts vs survey.

### Part 5: What the spending numbers cannot see (short)

14. **Is being above the line the same as being well?** No: 17.7% are poor once schooling,
    water, sanitation and electricity count (vs 5.3% on money alone in the same survey);
    35.5% of children under five were stunted (NFHS-5); about 31% faced hardship from
    health spending. · chart: *changed*, the deprivation chart trimmed to six bars.
15. **Ending:** what the next survey (and a national poverty line) would settle, and the
    sharpest point: India has lifted its floor, and the floor is still about Rs 68 a day.

## Questions and nuances the design missed (review, 3 November)

Each item is marked **sourced** (frozen already) or **to source** (must be opened and
frozen before use). Recommended additions are starred.

**Questions any reader would ask**

1. ★ **"If only 2% are extremely poor, why does the government give free grain to
   about 80 crore people?"** The most obvious lay objection, and the article never
   answers it. Covers what the grain is worth, and whether the World Bank's numbers
   count it (item 6). · to source (Department of Food and Public Distribution, PIB).
2. ★ **"What does an Indian family actually spend its money on?"** Food is now under half
   of spending: about 47% in rural India and 40% in urban India in 2023-24. That gives
   the reader something to picture before the poverty lines arrive. · sourced (HCES 2023-24 press note).
3. ★ **"How do the poorest live?"** The poorest 5% of rural Indians spent about
   Rs 1,677 a person a month in 2023-24, below the rough Rs 2,060 rupee equivalent of
   $3. That needs careful handling, because survey rupees are not price-adjusted the
   way the World Bank's are. · sourced (press note); the comparison needs a method note.
4. ★ **"Is $3 a day close to India's own old line?"** Tendulkar's 2011-12 lines
   (Rs 816 rural, Rs 1,000 urban a month), carried forward with CPI 2012=100, can be
   set beside the rupee value of $3. If they are close, the World Bank's "extreme"
   line is roughly India's last official line. · derivable from frozen data.
5. **"Has the gap between villages and cities closed?"** Urban spending was 84% above rural in
   2011-12 and 71% above in 2022-23. · sourced (HCES press note).

**Measurement nuances behind the World Bank's own numbers (the HCES debate)**

6. ★ **Does the World Bank's 2.3% count free food?** HCES publishes spending with and
   without imputed free items. Which one feeds PIP decides whether the headline
   includes the government's transfers, and that is the core of Bhalla's argument.
   · to source (PIP country notes, India brief methodology).
7. ★ **Which recall period?** The World Bank uses the "modified mixed recall"
   aggregate for India. The choice of recall window can move measured poverty
   (size to be sourced), and that was the 1999-2000 dispute all over again. · to source.
8. **One price level for rural and urban.** PIP now applies one national PPP to India,
   and leaves rural-urban price differences to the welfare aggregate. How this changed
   the rural-urban split since the 2017 PPP lines is not yet known. · partly sourced (PIP factor); history to source.
9. **Out-of-date weights.** HCES still weights households using the 2011 Census, so it
   likely understates the urban share (the brief says so). The next Census will reset
   them. · partly sourced (brief); Census timing to source.
10. **Who the survey misses:** the homeless, seasonal migrants, people in institutions.
    · to source (HCES coverage notes).

**Context and debate**

11. ★ **Income and wealth inequality.** The "fourth most equal" box should say what
    income and wealth data show, for example the World Inequality Lab's estimate of the
    top 1% income share. · to source (Bharti, Chancel, Piketty and Somanchi, 2024).
12. **Is spending rising on borrowed money?** RBI data on household debt and financial
    savings could test whether consumption gains rest on income or on credit. · to
    source (RBI; check `data/series` first).
13. **Who is still poor, by social group.** ST and SC households spend least. The HCES
    caste artifacts from the older poverty article already exist. · sourced.
14. **The calorie puzzle** (Deaton and Drèze, 2009): real spending rose while calorie
    intake fell. That is history explaining why calorie-based lines were abandoned. · to source.

Recommended: add 1-4, 6, 7 and 11. Items 5 and 13 fit into existing sections in a
sentence each. 8-10 go into the survey box. 12 and 14 only if the sources are strong.
