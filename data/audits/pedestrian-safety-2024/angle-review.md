# Pedestrian story: final angle review

Reviewed 26 September 2026 against the original *Road Accidents in India 2024* PDF, its lists of tables and annexures (PDF pp17–26), the audited source cells, the [WHO pedestrian-safety manual](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240072497), and the [19 June 2026 Supreme Court judgment](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2024/42514/42514_2024_3_1501_71888_Judgement_19-Jun-2026.pdf). This is an omission audit for the national fatality article, not a claim that every subject below can be measured nationally.

## Covered in the article

- Scale and road-user share: 36,526 pedestrian deaths, 20.6% of 177,175 road deaths (Table 4.4, PDF p98).
- Recent direction: direct 2023–24 pedestrian comparison. The report changed victim road-user classification from 2019; no pre-2019 series was silently joined (Table 4.4 and PDF p96).
- Age distribution and pedestrians' share of all road deaths within the same age band (Annexure 33, PDF p222; Table 4.2, PDF p92).
- Male/female pedestrian counts, within-sex road-death shares and the age-by-sex cross-check. The latter is higher for females in each of the six specified age bands; among victims aged 60 or older, 56.7% of females and 37.9% of males were pedestrians (Annexure 33, PDF p222; Table 4.3, PDF p93). These are victim-mix shares, never per-walk risk.
- Police-coded impacting vehicle and its large mixed residual (Table 4.5, PDF p100); state raw totals (Annexure 29(a), PDF p210); national highways versus all other roads by subtraction (Tables 2.11 and 4.4, PDF pp68 and 98).
- The Court's right-to-walk holding and local-authority duties. The 2024 deaths precede the 2026 ruling and cannot test compliance.

## Important gaps that the report cannot close for pedestrians

- **Exposure:** no comparable count of walking journeys, kilometres or time walked by age, sex, state or road class. Death counts, per-resident rates and shares of *road deaths* answer different questions from risk per walk. The 2024 [NSO Time Use Survey](https://microdata.gov.in/nada/index.php/catalog/236/related-materials) measures time spent in activities; it is not a verified, compatible count of walking journeys on roads to divide these fatalities by.
- **Place and time:** rural–urban fatalities (Table 7.1, PDF p138), junctions (Table 3.6, p81), weather (Table 3.8, p83), and time of day (Table 7.3, p142) are all-road tables. They do not cross-tabulate *pedestrian victims* with those fields. The million-plus-city tables likewise classify all road casualties, and Table 6.7's “Pedestrian” row is an *impacting-vehicle* code, not a city pedestrian-victim series (PDF pp127–128).
- **Street design:** the national table does not identify usable footpaths, crossings, road geometry, traffic speed or the precise site of each pedestrian death. The Court's ruling supplies a duty, not an inventory of compliance.
- **Nonfatal burden and other walking harms:** the pedestrian tables used here count deaths. They do not give a comparable national pedestrian injury, near-miss, accessibility or perceived-safety series. These require hospital, local crash, street-audit or survey evidence.
- **Cause and responsibility:** an impacting vehicle is a police classification, not a finding of fault. The all-road “hit and run” collision table (Table 1.5, PDF p40) cannot be applied to the pedestrian subset. Nor can all-road traffic-violation categories establish how many pedestrian deaths a specific behaviour caused.
- **Completeness:** police returns may miss deaths or fail to link later hospital deaths. WHO's different-vintage modelled road-death estimate is useful context in the broader road-safety article, but it cannot be mechanically converted into a 2024 pedestrian correction.
- **Disadvantage and access:** the national tables do not identify disability, income, caste, occupation, trip purpose or whether a child was walking to school. Their absence limits any equity claim beyond published age and male/female categories.

## Angles considered but not added as charts

- Annexure 13(B) subdivides national-highway authority categories, not walking exposure or footpath availability. It would repeat the road-class point without locating risk.
- Annexures 29, 29(a) and 33 allow more state-by-sex/age *counts*. Small-population and reporting differences make a national ranking easy to misread as walking danger. The current top-ten chart is labelled as a count of loss, not a safety league table.
- Earlier annual PDFs could support a carefully reconciled post-2019 series. This article makes only the 2023–24 comparison explicitly available in the 2024 report. A longer series needs a separate vintage-by-vintage audit and does not change the one-year age, sex or place limitations.
- International comparisons would mix national police returns with different registration practices and WHO model vintages. No like-for-like per-walk denominator was verified.

## Editorial result

Added the age-by-sex cross-check and an explicit fatal-crash scope statement. Clarified that the report's general road-crash geography and timing tables cannot locate pedestrian deaths. No further national chart earns its place from this source. The next reporting step is a city or district investigation joining geocoded crash and hospital records to a checked footpath/crossing inventory and measured walking exposure.
